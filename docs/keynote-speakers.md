## Prof. Felix Wolf

<img src="https://www.informatik.tu-darmstadt.de/media/informatik/fb20_allgemeines/fb20_profilfotos/fb20_proferssors/Wolf_Felix_2026_415x415.jpg" alt="Prof. Felix Wolf" width="320" height="320" style="width: 320px; height: 320px; object-fit: cover; object-position: center; display: block;" />

**Full Professor, Department of Computer Science**  
**Technical University of Darmstadt, Germany**

### Keynote Talk

**Title:** Rewiring the Human Brain: On the Fabric of Associative Thinking  
**Date:** Saturday, December 12, 2026  
**Time:** 09:30–10:15 (45 minutes)

### Abstract

Concept cells are neurons in the medial temporal lobe that represent an abstract concept, such as a familiar person, in a context-independent way. They are activated by heterogeneous and potentially multimodal sensory input related to the concept, such as viewing a photo of the person, reading the person's name, or hearing the person's voice. Learning the concept implies connecting the cortical cells that are triggered when such features are perceived with the cells of the concept, resulting in an associative memory engram. Traditional models explain its formation with Hebbian learning through synaptic plasticity, the strengthening of existing synapses in response to co-activation. However, the low edge density of the brain suggests that preexisting synapses between these cells are relatively unlikely, rendering such engrams inefficient and volatile. Instead, it is plausible that more persistent concept engrams rely on structural plasticity, involving the creation of synapses de novo. Yet, it remains unclear how neurons can find new postsynaptic targets over such a considerable distance. In this paper, we simulate the formation of such structural engrams in the connectomes of healthy subjects, offering a model hypothesis for how engrams can form between remote neurons by following homeostatic principles. We further demonstrate how activating a learned concept can trigger related concepts through overlapping sensory associations, in a process we call a percept-concept loop. Our results offer a new perspective on how learning could occur in the brain by adapting the network structure to changing tasks without central control.

### Biography

Felix Wolf is a full professor of computer science at the Technical University of Darmstadt, where he directs the High-Performance Computing Lab. His research focuses on methods, tools, and algorithms that support the development and deployment of HPC applications throughout their entire life cycle, including design, optimization, scaling, and tuning. Through his work on scalable algorithms for brain simulations, he developed an interest in computational neuroscience.

Prior to joining the Technical University of Darmstadt in 2015, he was a full professor at RWTH Aachen University and held positions at Forschungszentrum Jülich and the University of Tennessee in Knoxville. He received his Ph.D. from RWTH Aachen University in 2003. Wolf also heads NHR4CES@TUDa, a high-performance computing center within Germany's National High-Performance Computing Alliance (NHR).

## Assoc. Prof. Xueyan Tang

<img src="https://www3.ntu.edu.sg/home/asxytang/photo.jpg" alt="Assoc. Prof. Xueyan Tang" width="320" height="320" style="width: 320px; height: 320px; object-fit: cover; object-position: center; display: block;" />

**Associate Professor, College of Computing and Data Science**  
**Nanyang Technological University, Singapore**

### Keynote Talk

**Title:** Learning-Augmented Resource Management in Computer Systems  
**Date:** Saturday, December 12, 2026  
**Time:** 10:45–11:30 (45 minutes)

### Abstract

Efficient resource management is fundamental to computer systems, where decisions about allocating scarce resources such as computing capacity, memory, and bandwidth must often be made online without knowledge of future demands. Classical online algorithms typically optimize for worst-case scenarios, providing strong theoretical guarantees but often sacrificing practical efficiency. In contrast, real-world workloads exhibit predictable patterns that can be exploited to improve performance.

Learning-augmented algorithms offer a promising paradigm that combines the theoretical rigor of classical online algorithms with the predictive power of machine learning. By incorporating predictions into online decision-making, these algorithms aim to achieve near-optimal performance when predictions are accurate (consistency), degrade gracefully as prediction errors increase (smoothness), and retain strong worst-case guarantees even when predictions are unreliable (robustness).

In this talk, I will present our recent research on learning-augmented algorithms for two fundamental resource management problems: virtual machine placement and online caching. For virtual machine placement, we study dynamic bin packing algorithms that minimize the total usage time of physical machines in cloud data centers, exploring non-clairvoyant, clairvoyant, and learning-augmented settings with different levels of information about virtual machine lifetimes.

For online caching, we introduce a lightweight framework that strengthens the robustness of learning-augmented caching algorithms without compromising their optimal consistency or asymptotic time complexity, achieving the best-known consistency-robustness trade-off. Together, these studies illustrate how learning-augmented algorithm design can bridge the gap between worst-case theoretical guarantees and practical system efficiency.

### Biography

Xueyan Tang is an Associate Professor in the College of Computing and Data Science at Nanyang Technological University (NTU), Singapore. He received his Ph.D. in Computer Science from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in 2003 and his B.Eng. in Computer Science and Engineering from Shanghai Jiao Tong University in 1998.

His research interests include distributed systems, cloud computing, and resource allocation. He has served in editorial roles for leading journals, including *IEEE Transactions on Cloud Computing*, and has held program leadership and technical program committee roles for major international conferences in distributed and parallel computing.
